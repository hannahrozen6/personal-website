Investigating the Social Consequences of YouTube Watch-Time Optimization

  • Context: Analyzed a large-scale YouTube dataset (1.5 GB; ~600,000 videos) to examine how watch-time optimization influences exposure to health and political content. Created a focused subset of 22,193 videos by filtering for 22 health and political keywords associated with topics particularly vulnerable to misinformation and polarization. Investigated whether engagement-driven recommendation patterns sacrifice viewpoint diversity and contribute to informational “rabbit holes.”

  • Languages & Tools: Python - pandas & numpy for high-volume data engineering, scikit-learn for feature scaling and K-Means clustering, bertopic for semantic modeling, and matplotlib & seaborn for visualizations

  • Methodologies: Natural Language Processing (NLP), Unsupervised Machine Learning (Clustering & Topic Modeling), and Information Theory (Shannon Entropy)

  • My Contribution & Impact: I built and executed a machine learning and text analytics framework:

    1. Behavioral Clustering: Engineered data features combining log-transformed duration and engagement metrics (views, likes, comments) to run a K-Means algorithm (\(k=3\)). Identified three clear algorithmic archetypes: Low-Impact Snippet, Factual Rundown (dominating with 94% of total watch-time), and Rabbit Hole Perpetrator (averaging an immersive 25+ minute retention depth).
    2. Topic Modeling & Polarization Analysis: Coded a text-cleaning pipeline to extract custom-filtered auto-generated transcripts. Deployed BERTopic contextual embeddings to categorize dense ideological themes within the Rabbit Hole Perpetrator cluster.
    3. Quantifying Viewpoint Diversity: Applied the Shannon Information-Theory Entropy formula to compute a Topic Entropy score of 2.80 and an evenness index of 0.84 within the long-form deep-dive cluster. Empirically demonstrated that despite topic distribution, the broader ecosystem isolates users into highly concentrated narrative silos.

Finding the Best Schools for Your Money: A Data-Driven Analysis of College ROI

  • Context: Compiled and analyzed a comprehensive dataset combining institutional demographic and financial data with state economic contexts. Evaluated short- and long-term financial returns across 1,173 four-year U.S. universities and examined the impact of regional employment rate on college return on investment (ROI).

  • Languages & Tools: R — tidyverse (dplyr for data manipulation & ggplot2 for scatterplot regression visualization), knitr for LaTeX summary tables, and Quarto for reproducible reporting

  • Methodologies: Long-Term Financial Metric Modeling, Macro-Environmental Control Analysis, and Bivariate Ordinary Least Squares Regression

  • Outcome & Impact: Developed a multi-tier financial framework calculating 1-year baseline payback periods, 5-year and 10-year wage premiums, and 5-year cumulative returns on investment. Uncovered a stark contrast in the types of institutions at opposite ends of the financial returns gap. Affordable public institutions achieved the highest cumulative returns on investment, whereas expensive private liberal arts colleges in the bottom tier experienced negative early-career returns. Using regression analysis, I found that ROI is largely independent of the state employment rate and is much more likely to be impacted by institutional traits.

Targeting the 0.1%: Mitigating Emergency Department Over-Utilization

  • Context: Collaborated in a 48-hour data hackathon to analyze a large-scale regional healthcare dataset representing millions of patient records. Our team targeted emergency department “super-utilizers,” identifying that the top 1000 patients (0.1% of the patient population) accounted for 10.5% of all emergency department visits.

  • Languages & Tools: R - data.table for memory-optimized loading, tidyverse (dplyr for data wrangling, tidyr for data reshaping, and ggplot2 for data visualization)

  • Methodologies: Data Preprocessing and Statistical Significance Testing (Two-Sample T-Test)

  • My Contribution & Impact: I engineered a data pipeline to classify patients into geographic regions (corridors) based on FIPS codes. I then coded two primary analytical components:

    1. The ‘Medical Commute’ Analysis: Visualized the percentage of clinic visits occurring outside patients’ home regions and graphed the reliance on the emergency department by corridor. After comparing, I concluded that a lack of local clinic access correlates with emergency room dependency.
    2. Statistical Significance Testing: Conducted a Two-Sample T-Test confirming a statistically significant difference (\(p = 0.0185\)) in emergency reliance scores between Central and Non-Central patient cohorts, providing our team with the empirical support to propose expanding targeted clinic infrastructure to vulnerable corridors.
